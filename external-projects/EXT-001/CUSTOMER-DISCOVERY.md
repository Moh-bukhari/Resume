## Prepare a conversation

Choose five to seven questions for roughly 30 minutes. Follow one significant
incident deeply; the lists below are a menu, not a script to complete.

Before the conversation, write down the assumptions you want to test. For
example: a particular handoff frequently interrupts work; the team spends
meaningful effort recovering missing context; or a manager has authority
and budget to change that process. Each is a hypothesis until supported.

Start with the person's work and a recent shift or project phase. Keep the
AI and wearable pitch out of the discovery portion. An opening could be:

> I’m researching how work actually gets done on construction projects,
> especially where it becomes difficult or interrupted. Could we walk through
> a recent example from your work?

Where possible, speak with workers separately from their supervisors so they
can describe the process candidly. Ask before recording. Request nonconfidential
examples and avoid collecting names or project details that are unnecessary
for understanding the problem.

## Six questions for the main conversation

1. **What were you trying to accomplish during your most recent shift or week?**
   Establish the work, people involved, project phase, and expected result.

2. **Which part was hardest to get done? Walk me through the latest occurrence.**
   Anchor general complaints to an actual event. If the answer is “communication,”
   ask what information was missing, when it became necessary, and who needed it.

3. **What happened between the first sign of the problem and work continuing?**
   Follow the sequence: who noticed, what they did, who they contacted, what
   each person was waiting for, and who could resolve it.

4. **What happened to the work while that was being sorted out?**
   Investigate waiting, switching tasks, repeating work, idle equipment,
   overtime, and downstream effects. Ask how the person knows the impact.

5. **When else has something similar happened recently?**
   Obtain another example and a bounded period. Separate a recurring issue
   from one memorable event, and check whether the causes were actually similar.

6. **What has the team already tried to address it, and what happened?**
   Explore the current workaround, effort, purchases, and abandoned approaches.
   Ask to see a nonconfidential example of the process when useful.

## Questions for construction workers

Use these to understand what happens at the point of work:

- The last time you needed an answer before continuing, how did you obtain it,
  and what did you do while waiting?
- Describe the last piece of work that had to be redone. When was the issue
  discovered, and what information was available beforehand?
- At the end of your last shift, what did the next person need to know, and
  how did you pass that along?
- What did an experienced person recently have to explain to a newer worker?
  How was that knowledge shared afterward?
- What did you have to record during the last shift? Show me the steps and
  where recording interrupted the work, if it did.

Avoid asking workers to judge whether their employer would buy a product.
Their firsthand account establishes workflow and consequences; investigate
budget and purchasing authority with the people who actually hold them.

## Questions for managers

Use these to connect incidents to project consequences and change authority:

- Which unplanned issue required the most attention last week? How did it
  affect the schedule, and what records show that effect?
- During the last handoff between crews, how did you establish that the
  preceding work was ready for the next team?
- Which information did you have to chase most recently? Walk me through
  how you found it and what depended on it.
- Tell me about the last tool or process change introduced for a similar
  problem. Who requested it, approved it, paid for it, and used it?
- What made that change stick, or what prevented adoption?
- Which parties would have needed to agree to change the process in the
  incident we discussed: your team, a subcontractor, the client, or another owner?

Distinguish the person experiencing the problem, the process owner, the
budget owner, and the approver. They may belong to different organizations.
Existing software, contractual responsibilities, or incentives may explain
why an apparently simple process change has not happened.

## Questions for Professor Shami

Use the professor's perspective to identify patterns and reach practitioners:

- On a megaproject you personally worked on, which recurring problem caused
  significant disruption? Could we unpack one incident?
- What seemed to be the problem initially, and what turned out to be the cause?
- Where did different contractors' responsibilities meet, and what happened
  at that boundary in this example?
- Can you describe a comparable project where the process worked well? What
  was different about the people, incentives, tools, or project conditions?
- Who handled this issue day to day, and could you introduce me?

Record whether an example is firsthand, reported by someone else, or a broader
industry impression. Expert perspective can direct the search; it does not
establish that a particular customer will buy.

## Test whether the apparent opportunity is real

Ask questions that could change the direction of the product:

- What other problem has caused more trouble recently?
- When did this process work smoothly? What made that possible?
- What has prevented the team from addressing it?
- What was already available that could have helped in that incident?
- Did better information resolve it, or did something else have to change?

Do not force every incident into a communication or wearable problem. A delay
caused by unavailable materials may require a different intervention from
a delay caused by an unnoticed delivery. An approval wait may reflect required
inspection capacity rather than a missing notification.

No workaround does not automatically mean no pain. A person may lack authority,
time, budget, or an acceptable alternative. Equally, a complaint alone does
not establish a high-priority purchase.

## Evidence and costing

Useful artifacts include a redacted message sequence, drawing revision,
schedule, handoff note, issue log, or an observed workflow. Request access
only when the person can appropriately share it.

Keep measured figures, recorded estimates, and informal recollections separate.
If someone says an issue cost a day, ask whose day, how many people were affected,
what they did instead, and whether the overall schedule changed. Avoid adding
overlapping crew-time and project-delay costs as though they were independent.

Record the original sequence before proposing a technical explanation. A
general statement such as “we always wait on approvals” is a lead to investigate,
not a quantified finding.

## Close with a concrete next step

Choose a request that follows naturally from the incident:

- Could I observe the next occurrence of that handoff?
- Could you show me a redacted example of the record you mentioned?
- Who handles the other side of this process, and could you introduce me?
- May I return after comparing this with another team's experience?

Introductions, observation access, and shared examples are evidence of
engagement. They are not proof of willingness to pay. Discuss a paid evaluation
when there is a sufficiently specific problem, proposed test, and appropriate
budget owner; do not record a hypothetical offer as a commitment.

## Interview note template

Use a separate note per interview. Leave unknown fields unknown. Keep private
recordings and identifiable project material out of a public repository;
publish only appropriately redacted summaries.

| Field | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Date and interview status | Planned or completed; actual date when known. |
| Participant context | Role, organization type, project type/scale/phase, and firsthand scope. |
| Work and expected result | The recurring task or handoff being examined. |
| Specific incident | Sequence of events in the participant's account. |
| Recurrence | Another incident and the period covered; distinguish estimates. |
| Consequence | Waiting, rework, delay, equipment impact, or other result. |
| Cost evidence | Recorded value, calculation, estimate, or unknown; avoid double counting. |
| Existing response | Current process, tools, staff effort, spending, and previous attempts. |
| Ownership | Person affected, process owner, purchaser, approver, and other parties. |
| Supporting evidence | Redacted artifact, observation, exact quote, or reported account. |
| Counterevidence | Cases where the problem was absent or solved by existing means. |
| Next step | Specific agreed action, responsible person, and known timing. |
| Researcher's interpretation | Hypothesis clearly separated from what the person actually said. |

## Compare interviews and choose the first workflow

Group interviews by comparable roles, organizations, work, and project conditions.
Do not average unrelated construction, laboratory, and space operations into
one supposed customer. Preserve the difference between “not mentioned,” “unknown,”
and “explicitly not a problem.”

Look for a recurring incident with a meaningful consequence, a process owner,
existing effort to address it, and access to evaluate an improvement. Repeated
examples and costly action are stronger signals than enthusiasm for the idea.

Write a short evidence-backed statement for each candidate workflow:

> This type of team encounters this problem during this recurring task.
> These incidents show the consequence. This is how the team currently handles
> it, who owns the outcome, and what evidence we can obtain next.

There is no universal interview count or numerical score that establishes
demand. Pursue unresolved contradictions, look for examples outside a friendly
network, and test the strongest problem with observation and a bounded pilot.
The selected V10 vision remains the context; discovery determines which part
of that vision can first solve a customer's demonstrated problem.
