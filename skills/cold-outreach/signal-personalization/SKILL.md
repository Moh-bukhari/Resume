# Signal personalization: one real signal beats ten mail-merge fields

Reply rates in the source playbook's benchmarks tell the whole story:
cold messages with no signal and no personalization sit around 1-3%;
a single real signal, personalized, sits around 18-22%; three or more
stacked signals reach 35-40%. Personalization is not decoration. It is
the mechanism that makes a stranger reply, and it only works when the
signal is real, recent, and connected to the message.

## The signal ladder

Grade every draft's personalization before it goes out. Only levels
2 and 3 are real personalization.

- **Level 0, fake:** company name only, job title only, industry
  ("I see you work in AI"). Every automation tool does this. It reads
  as automation and buys nothing.
- **Level 1, thin:** a true but public fact anyone could find in ten
  seconds (funding round, headcount, "growing fast"). Usable only as
  context, never as the hook.
- **Level 2, real:** something the person themselves said, shipped, or
  did. A post, a talk, a project, a hiring decision, a team change.
  Dated and checkable. This is the minimum bar for any message.
- **Level 3, sharp:** a level-2 signal plus the consequence you draw
  from it for their seat. "Your post ranked experience last in hiring"
  is level 2. "That is a bar I would rather be measured against than a
  resume" is level 3. The inference is what makes the message
  unmistakably yours; nobody else's template produces it.

Rules:

- One signal per message. Two signals compete; three signals read as a
  dossier.
- The signal must be recent enough to matter: a post within about 30
  days, a hiring signal within about 30 days, a company event within
  about 90 days. Older signals need a reason they are still live.
- Never invent a signal. If no level-2 signal exists for a person, say
  so in the research notes and lead with a level-1 company fact plus a
  sharper proof point, or skip the person. A guessed detail is worse
  than a plain one.
- Stack signals across touches, not within one message. Touch one uses
  the post; touch two uses the hiring signal; touch three uses the team
  change. Each touch then feels researched rather than repeated.

## Signal types worth hunting

1. **Their content:** post, article, talk, comment. Strongest, because
   it is their own words and it dates itself.
2. **Hiring signal:** a role they posted, a team they are building, a
   "we are hiring" post. It names the constraint directly.
3. **Role change:** they joined, moved teams, or were promoted. New
   seats mean open problems and short patience for generic mail.
4. **Company event:** launch, partnership, migration, reorg. Use only
   when you can tie it to their seat; otherwise it is level 1.
5. **Workflow or stack change:** a tool they adopted, a system they
   replaced, a process they described. Gold for practitioner contacts,
   because it names their day.
6. **Tenure milestone:** years at the company, anniversary of a team.
   Weakest; use only when nothing else exists.

## Altitude: above the line vs below the line

- **Above the line (founders, VPs, directors):** write about outcomes
  they own: the constraint on the team, the hire that unblocks a roadmap,
  the system that stops leaking time. Keep it shortest; they read on a
  phone between meetings.
- **Below the line (managers, ICs, practitioners):** write about their
  day: the workflow, the tool, the friction they personally feel. Peer
  language, not vendor language. Proof framed as time saved, friction
  removed, or errors eliminated, never as revenue or margins.

## Sequencing: every touch adds a new angle

- **Touch 1:** introduce. Signal plus consequence plus one proof point
  plus one small ask. 60-90 words for email.
- **Touch 2 (3-5 days later, same thread):** new angle, new context.
  Never repeat touch 1. A different proof point, or the same proof
  applied to a different part of their problem.
- **Touch 3:** lower friction. Accept the no gracefully, offer something
  useful (a short note, a relevant observation), and leave the door open.
  No guilt, no "just bumping this to the top of your inbox."
- **One person, one live message at a time.** If the same person is on
  two lists, one message is live and the other is held. Never send two
  messages to the same person in the same week.
- **Plain text only.** No HTML, no images, no tracked links in a cold
  message. Verify every email address before sending; a bounce costs
  more than a skipped send.

## Personalization check (run on every draft)

1. Could this opening line be sent to anyone else at the company? If
   yes, it is level 0 or 1. Rewrite or replace the signal.
2. Does the message name something the person said or did, with a
   date or a detail only a reader would know? If no, it is not ready.
3. Is there exactly one proof point, and does it match the signal's
   consequence? If the proof could attach to any signal, it is generic.
4. Is there exactly one ask, answerable in a sentence?
5. Would the reader know, within three seconds, why this message is
   for them specifically? If not, cut the first paragraph and start
   again from the signal.

## Example (job-seeker voice, level-3 signal)

> Subject: Grab rollout
>
> Your June post on deploying the Grab partnership across countries
> and languages is the hardest version of the forward-deployed job:
> scale, languages, and live customers at once. I applied for Founders
> Initiatives on Friday.
>
> At Tamara I built a recruiting CRM on Greenhouse plus OpenAI search
> over 700K+ records that TA and the COO actually run on. Internal
> systems only count if the people running the company keep using
> them, which is why I am writing to you rather than to a queue.
>
> What separates the people who succeed in these seats?

## Anti-patterns

- Mail-merge personalization: `{{first_name}}`, `{{company}}`, and a
  compliment. Readers have seen it a thousand times.
- Fake familiarity: "loved your recent post" with no detail from the
  post. Worse than no personalization, because it claims research that
  did not happen.
- Signal dumping: three facts in the opener to prove you researched.
  It reads as surveillance, not attention.
- Repeating touch 1 in touch 2 with "just checking in." A follow-up
  that adds nothing teaches the reader that your next message will add
  nothing either.
- Pitching the wrong altitude: strategy to a practitioner, tooling
  minutiae to a founder.
